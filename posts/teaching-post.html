My first teaching experience

November 27, 2026

I remember walking into Fremont Hall at Midland University, eager to teach my very first class the foundations of coaching. I introduced myself and got into the lesson plan for the semester. Everything went over swimmingly, but once class dismissed I remembered something. I forgot to take attendance.

This was a teaching role I had taken on short notice, and I couldn't even fathom what I had gotten myself into after the first day. I knew I had to prepare better for the rest of the semester if I wanted to teach the class valuable coaching lessons.

What was going on?

The first couple lessons were reading off another teacher's PowerPoint and trying to add in my own experiences and beliefs in some situational lessons. I started to notice students falling asleep, getting sidetracked, or just not being present in class. I thought my approach on lecturing the class with some assignments sprinkled in-between was the right move. But there was little to no interaction aside from a few students.

I looked into our teaching portal and realized something. Half of my class didn't even want to be coaches. To some this was a filler course for some extra credits, and the specificity that I was teaching with didn't really apply to many of them.

A new approach

I knew I had to make a change, and fast. The last thing I wanted to do was waste the students time in a class they felt had no meaning. So I improvised and created my own presentations to teach the class.

I began going over the lessons from a coaching perspective but midway through each class, I'd show them how these rules and lessons can apply to other jobs and positions. The pressures and challenges a coach deals with can apply to many jobs. Sometimes you aren't training and worrying about athletes, you are working with other employees, figuring out issues on your own, and sometimes you have to step up and lead even when you don't want to.

I also realized that this class is not about me, but the lessons. I started implementing more group work and allowing the students to fix situations I provided to them. Students who sat in the back and wouldn't speak were asked to present for their groups in certain situations and later on, they were participating in class without anyone telling them to.

Participation was at an all-time high towards the end of the semester, and in our final papers I pushed for alternative perspectives in high-pressure situations. As I read their papers, I was very impressed with what they came up with. Different ways of handling tough situations, and even some approaches that I never thought of or taught them.

Conclusion

Whether the students knew everything before class even started, or they learned it through the course, what I saw as the biggest change was the effort they gave in class. Fewer late assignments, more presence in the classroom, and overall they were actually learning, to the point their application in the assignments was very different from student to student.

They did more than the bare minimum, which is all I could ever ask for as a teacher. They didn't just walk through this course, they trained with it and the results showed. Where they go in life is a mystery, but they have the tools to get things done and not be fazed by the pressure that unfolds and be leaders wherever they go.
